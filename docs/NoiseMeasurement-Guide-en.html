Noise Measurement Guide

Which noise ApranatNR, DarkPixelPlot and FlatSmooth measure, how they measure it, and how they decide. It also covers how the results were compared during testing.

1. What is measured

The three tools use the same word, "noise", for four different things. Each one builds a reference for how much a normal pixel should vary, then looks for pixels or amounts that fall outside it.

QuantityDirection of the scatterImage usedTool
Dark fluctuationThe same pixel, frame to frame (temporal)Individual darksApranatNR (drunk pixels), DarkPixelPlot
Scatter in one frameA pixel against its same-colour neighbours (spatial)One calibrated lightApranatNR (star protection)
Scatter in a stackSame, with the random noise averaged downUnregistered averageApranatNR (cool file, faint-star protection)
Flat pixel noiseRelative difference from the surroundings (%)Master flatFlatSmooth

Temporal scatter tells how differently a pixel reads each time; spatial scatter tells how different it is from its neighbours. Dark fluctuation can only be measured in time: a master dark is an average and has lost it. Conversely, patterns fixed to the sensor (cool pixels, the grain of a flat) are the same in every frame, so they are invisible in time and are measured in space.

2. Common tools

2.1 An outlier-resistant sigma (1.4826 × MAD)

A plain standard deviation is pulled up by a few large values such as stars or defects. Almost every measurement here therefore uses a robust sigma:

MAD   = median( |value − median(value)| )
sigma = 1.4826 × MAD

1.4826 makes it equal to the standard deviation for normally distributed data. Even when almost half of the values are outliers, it still describes the scatter of the normal pixels.

Unless stated otherwise, "5σ" and "3σ" below use this robust sigma. For a normal distribution about one pixel in 3 million lies beyond 5σ, so a pixel beyond 5σ is almost certainly not normal.

2.2 Comparing same-colour pixels only

In an undebayered colour (Bayer) image, adjacent pixels are different colours. R and G differ in brightness, so comparing them directly would count the colour difference as noise. Comparisons therefore use only the same-colour pixels two pixels apart, and sigma is computed separately for each of the four positions (R, G, G, B). Mono images use the adjacent pixels.

2.3 Difference from the neighbour median (residual)

Spatial scatter is measured as each pixel's difference from the median of its 8 same-colour neighbours: the residual.

  • Median, not mean: one hot pixel among the neighbours barely moves the median.
  • The pixel itself is excluded: with a 3×3 median that includes the pixel, every pixel that happens to be the median gets a residual of exactly 0, and a sigma computed from residuals piled up at 0 comes out too small.
  • Defects are excluded: when repairing drunk pixels, neighbours in the defect map are left out of the median (with fewer than 3 left, the pixel is not repaired).

2.4 Subsampling

Sigmas and medians are computed on evenly spaced samples to save time (at most 1 million pixels in ApranatNR and DarkPixelPlot, 200,000 per colour for repair protection, every third row and column in FlatSmooth). An even stride would pick a single colour, so strides are made odd. On images of millions of pixels the effect on the result is negligible.

3. Dark fluctuation (drunk pixels)

Input: individual darks (8 or more, ideally about 20). ApranatNR's defect map and DarkPixelPlot's plot use the same computation.

3.1 Fluctuation and level of each pixel

  1. Trimmed mean and trimmed standard deviation: for each pixel, the highest and the lowest value over all frames are dropped, then the mean and standard deviation are computed. A pixel hit by one cosmic ray is then not mistaken for one that always fluctuates.
  2. Lower value (level): the pixel's dark level is trimmed mean − 0.6745 × trimmed std, the lower quartile of a normal distribution. A drunk pixel that sometimes jumps upward has its mean raised by the jumps, but its lower value stays at its usual level. Compared by the mean, a jumping pixel would be taken for a pixel with high dark current (this happened with Canon EOS 6D darks).

3.2 Expected fluctuation (physics model)

A normal pixel fluctuates by its read noise plus the shot noise of its dark current. Shot-noise variance is proportional to the signal, so:

expected² = read noise² + slope × max(0, level − normal level)
  • Normal level and read noise: the medians of the levels and of the fluctuations over all pixels. Almost all pixels are normal, so the medians are the values of a normal pixel.
  • Slope: for warm pixels, at least 10 read-noise units above the normal level, (fluctuation² − read noise²) / (level − normal level) is computed, and the median is taken. With fewer than 50 warm pixels the slope is 0 (constant fluctuation).
  • Why a model: earlier versions joined the median fluctuation of level groups into a curve. Away from the normal level, a larger share of the pixels are noisy ones, so the curve rose by itself and missed defects; beyond the brightest hot pixel the curve stopped, and judgement there went wrong. The physics model is not raised by noisy pixels, however many there are, and extends to any level.
  • Checking the slope: the slope should be proportional to the ADU per electron (the gain). On the ASI533MC Pro it was 2.7 at Gain 100, 85 at 400 and 165 at 460. ZWO gain is in units of 0.1 dB, so 100→400 is 30 dB (×31.6) and 400→460 is 6 dB (×2.0), which matches the slope ratios (×31, ×1.9).

3.3 Decision

Pixels whose fluctuation exceeds 4 × expected (1.5 to 20 in the settings) go into the defect map as drunk pixels. The expected fluctuation has a floor of 0.5 ADU in 16-bit units, so that clipped darks do not flag every pixel that moves at all.

One set of darks does not find them all. Drunk pixels come and go, so one set of 24 darks finds about 60% of them. A map built from darks of another day agrees on 60–70% of its pixels, against about 1% by chance, so the pixels found are real defects.

4. Scatter in one light (star protection)

Replacing a defect by its neighbour median dims a star whose core sits on it. The change made by the replacement is therefore compared with the scatter of that frame.

  1. Frame sigma: in one calibrated light, the robust sigma of the residuals (2.3) of pixels outside the defect map, per colour. It is that frame's pixel-to-pixel scatter: sky shot noise plus read noise.
  2. Decision: a defect whose value would change by more than 5σ is not repaired. A drunk-pixel jump stays within a few sigma of the sky noise; a star core is far brighter.

A faint star buried in the noise of one frame escapes this test. So the unregistered stack of the unrepaired lights is checked as well (the per-pixel average without the brightest frame, so cosmic rays are not taken for stars). Without dithering a star stays on the same pixels and stands out clearly in the stack.

  • Own excess: in the stack, the pixel's residual exceeds 5σ (sigma from the stack, same method).
  • Footprint: the residuals of the 8 adjacent pixels (any colour), in sigma units z, each capped at 6, give Σz / √(number of pixels) above 3. Starlight spreads to adjacent pixels; a drunk-pixel residual stays on one pixel. The cap keeps one adjacent hot pixel from meeting the condition alone.

It protects too much in low-noise frames. In darks with no sky noise, or short narrowband exposures, the drunk-pixel jumps themselves exceed 5σ, and background defects are left unrepaired.

5. The stacked image (cool file)

Averaging the lights without registration lowers the random noise by √(number of frames), and blurs the stars if the field drifts a little. Only dark spots fixed to the sensor (cool pixels) stay in place.

  1. The residual (difference from the median of the 8 same-colour neighbours) of every pixel of the stack is computed, with its median and robust sigma per colour.
  2. Pixels with a residual below min(0, median) − k × sigma are cool pixels (k = 5 by default, 0 to 10 in the settings). The reference never goes above 0, so k = 0 means exactly "every pixel darker than its neighbours" (the article's "threshold zero").
  3. Structure protection: where the range of the 8 neighbours (maximum − minimum, without the brightest and the darkest one) exceeds 5σ, the pixel lies on a star image or a nebula edge and is not corrected. Only isolated dark spots on flat ground are.
  4. The difference from the surroundings (−residual) is stored in the cool file and added to that pixel in every frame.

6. Pixel noise of the master flat (FlatSmooth)

6.1 Why flat noise matters

Every light is divided by the flat. A relative pixel noise σflat in the flat stamps every light with the same pattern, of size brightness × σflat, at the same sensor position. Random noise falls as 1/√N when frames are stacked; this pattern does not fall at all.

master noise² ≈ (random noise of one frame)² / N + fixed pattern²

If the field does not move, the pattern stays as it is. If it drifts slowly, registration stretches the pattern along the drift into the streaks of walking noise.

In that data set the flat signal after dark subtraction (about 0.003) was dimmer than the light background (about 0.012), and the master flat had 6–9% pixel noise. 0.012 × 6% ≈ 7e-4, close to the 5.8e-4 fixed pattern measured in the lights.

6.2 How it is measured

  1. The master flat is split by colour (R, G, G, B for a Bayer mosaic; each channel for mono or RGB).
  2. For each pixel, the relative difference from its 5×5 local mean, (pixel − local mean) / local mean. Dividing by the local mean leaves out slow changes such as vignetting and dust shadows.
  3. The robust sigma of these differences (every third row and column), divided by √(1 − 1/25): the pixel itself makes up 1/25 of the local mean, which makes the difference slightly too small.
  4. The largest value over the colours is the flat's pixel noise (the colour with the least light, usually B, is the noisiest).

6.3 Choosing the smoothing radius

Smoothing applies a box mean of radius r three times within each colour (three passes approximate a Gaussian blur). White noise is reduced by the square root of the sum of the squared weights. In 2-D this is the product of the two directions, so the sum of the squared 1-D weights is the reduction factor itself.

Radius r (same-colour pixels)Noise factorBlur width (FWHM, sensor pixels)

Automatic mode picks the smallest radius with pixel noise × factor ≤ target (0.3% by default). If the pixel noise is already below the target, nothing is done. Examples: the Canon flat (8.9% at most) gets r = 8; the synthetic flat of the WBPP end-to-end test (1.85%) gets r = 2 and ends at 0.21%.

Before smoothing, pixels more than 4σ from the median of their 8 same-colour neighbours (hot or dead pixels, values at or below 0, NaN) are replaced by that median; blurring them as they are would spread them. At the image edges the neighbours are extrapolated linearly, so the vignetting slope does not bias the test there.

What is lost: pixel-to-pixel sensitivity differences (PRNU) and dust shadows smaller than the blur width are no longer corrected. A noisy flat cannot measure them anyway. With a good flat (well exposed, 20+ frames), automatic mode does nothing.

7. Comparing results

These are the methods used to check the effect of ApranatNR and FlatSmooth. Each compares a master made with a step against one made without it. They are test computations, not part of the tools.

7.1 What to align first

  • Reference frame and position: WBPP may pick a different reference frame on each run. A shifted master is aligned by phase correlation. Interpolation changes the noise, so a comparison with different reference frames is only indicative.
  • Brightness scale: WBPP may rescale its output (one real case: ×1.049 + 0.010). Match the scales with a straight-line fit before comparing.
  • Drop the borders: borders covered by fewer frames because of drift are noisier and differ easily.
  • Mask the stars: for background measurements, pixels whose high-pass (image − 13×13 mean) exceeds 3σ are stars; the mask is grown a little.

7.2 Background noise

  • Fine noise: the robust sigma of image − 3×3 mean, without stars: pixel-scale noise.
  • Mid noise: the robust sigma of 3×3 mean − 17×17 mean: the scale of walking-noise streaks.
  • Both are divided by the background level and compared as relative values. A figure such as "background noise −1.7%" is this ratio.

7.3 Direction of walking noise

For the mid band, the correlation between pixels 4 pixels apart is computed in 24 directions. Ordinary noise is the same in every direction; walking noise has a much higher correlation along the drift (along) than across it (across).

7.4 Size and origin of a fixed pattern

  • Odd versus even frames: the high-pass of each calibrated light (difference from the same-colour 5×5 mean, stars masked) is median-stacked separately for odd and even frames. Random noise is unrelated between the two; a pattern fixed to the sensor appears in both. The square root of their covariance is the size of the fixed pattern, and their correlation its share (Canon example: correlation 0.75).
  • Finding the cause: correlate that pattern with the high-pass of the master flat (relative). In the Canon example it was −0.82: pixels where the flat is brighter are darker in the lights, the trace of the flat division. The same test against the master dark rules candidates in or out one by one.
  • Fixed to the sensor or to the sky: build the same pattern from the first and the last frames and correlate them at a range of shifts. If the correlation peaks at zero shift, the pattern is on the sensor (sky objects would peak at the shift of the drift).

7.5 Defect traces

At the positions in the defect map, count the share of pixels whose residual in the master exceeds 5σ (or 3σ). Count the same share at as many defect-free positions (the control). When the defect positions come down to the control level, no trace remains. Example, unguided and undithered data: 1.75% without correction, 1.50% with ApranatNR 1.5.2, 1.63% for the control.

7.6 Star brightness

Star brightness can be measured like this: for each star, the sum in an aperture of radius 4 (9×9 pixels) minus the median of a 25×25 area times the pixel count (the background), then the ratio with step ÷ without step. Look at the median ratio and its 5–95% range. For faint stars, stars whose core sits on a defect were compared with stars whose core does not (the control) to check that star protection works.

8. Reading the numbers

  • 1.4826 × MAD assumes a normal distribution: when the distribution is far from normal (quantized values, for example), the sigma itself differs from the standard deviation. Decisions compare values measured with the same yardstick, so this rarely matters.
  • Compare small differences with a control: a 1–2% difference in background noise can also come from a different reference frame or interpolation. Judge it from a comparison where only the step differs, or next to a control such as defect-free positions.
  • Do not mix colours: a sigma computed over mixed colours of an undebayered image includes the colour differences and comes out too large. After debayering, measure on the luminance (mean of the channels).
  • Temperature drift in darks: when the temperature changes during a dark set, the dark current of hot pixels changes, and their fluctuation exceeds the model. A few dozen hot pixels may then be flagged as drunk pixels.
  • Leftover files: WBPP remembers the previous file lists. Before comparing, check IC.masterDarkPath and the Master flat: lines in the log to confirm the intended dark and flat were used.
